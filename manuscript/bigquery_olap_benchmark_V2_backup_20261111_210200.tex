\textbf{\color{springerblue}Abstract}\\[0.3em]
\textbf{Background:} Analytical engines span serverless cloud, vectorized CPUs, and GPUs, yet no single engine dominates across scales ($\text{SF}\in\{1, 10, 30, 100\}\text{ GB}$): simple scans incur GPU PCIe and cloud overheads, window/unnest pipelines bottleneck CPUs, and large working sets trigger streaming or disk spilling.

\textbf{Methodology:} We propose \textbf{AST-Route}, an interpretable, risk-aware query router. Layer~1 extracts a 9D vector ($\mathbf{x}_q\in\mathbb{R}^9$, or $\mathbf{x}_q^{\text{scale}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$ with $\log_{10}(1+\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{scan}})$) combining AST counts, a Composite Structural Complexity Index ($\text{CSCI}$, 10,000-trial $\bar{\rho}=0.9880$; $\rho_{\text{partial}}=0.5477, p=3.68\times 10^{-7}$; $\text{VIF}=1.84$), and zone-map selectivity $\hat{\sigma}(q)$ ($0.172\text{ ms}$ cached, $0.413\text{ ms}$ cold footer). Layers~2--4 pair crossover models with Ridge, shallow trees, $k$-NN, in-fold rules, and a Gradient Boosting Regressor ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$) with 90\% conformal intervals ($\text{ECE}=1.78\%$) and a $12\%$ guard across SQLite~3, BigQuery, DuckDB, Polars, DataFusion, and an RTX~2050 GPU.

\textbf{Results:} Across 163 queries (75 factorial TPC-H, 22 Q1--Q22, 33 JOB, 13 SSB, 20 ClickBench), CPUs (Polars $82.47\text{ ms}$, DataFusion $96.30\text{ ms}$, DuckDB $183.74\text{ ms}$; active energy $6.40\text{ J}$ DuckDB, $3.17\text{ J}$ Polars) beat SQLite~3 ($8417.33\text{ ms}$), while RTX~2050 reaches $56.96\text{ ms}$ cold ($1.45\times$ vs.\ Polars, $3.23\times$ vs.\ DuckDB) and $22.25\text{ ms}$ warm ($3.71\times$ vs.\ Polars, $8.22\times$ kernel). Under 25-seed LOSO-CV ($\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$), Ridge ($49.12\text{ ms}$), Shallow DT ($51.35\text{ ms}$), $k$-NN ($52.84\text{ ms}$), and In-Fold Rules ($53.07\text{ ms}$) minimize mean latency at high P95/P99 regret ($150.9\%\text{--}196.8\%$ / $268.4\%\text{--}342.1\%$), whereas conformal AST-Route ($54.92\text{ ms}$ warm, $55.16\text{ ms}$ cold footer, $57.33\%$ Top-1, $76.00\%$ Top-2, $34.98\%$ mean regret, $1.381\text{ J}$ estimated energy) cuts P95/P99 regret to $114.11\%$/$218.40\%$ (vs.\ $257.31\%$ GPU) and halves $>100\%$ regret failures ($14.67\%\rightarrow 6.67\%$). Under seed-level ($N=25$) Holm tests, AST-Route beats DuckDB ($3.35\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=5.45\times 10^{-4}$), DataFusion ($1.75\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0041$), Polars ($1.50\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0171$), and Rule-Based ($p=0.0187$), matching Always GPU in mean latency ($p=0.184$). CPU-only routing ($M_{\text{gpu\_max}}=0$) achieves $74.67\%$ Top-1 ($69.12\text{ ms}$) and $86.67\%$ Top-1 ($62.05\text{ ms}$) on a 16-core node. It transfers to unseen seeds ($73.33\%$ Top-1, $47.01\text{ ms}$), Q1--Q22 ($68.18\%$ Top-1, $90.91\%$ Top-2, $16.98\text{ ms}$), SSB ($92.31\%$ Top-1, $12.15\text{ ms}$), ClickBench ($80.00\%$ Top-1, $24.18\text{ ms}$), JOB ($6.75\text{ ms}$), $C=1\text{--}32$ ($154.44\text{ QPS}$), and $\text{SF}=1\text{--}100\text{ GB}$ ($3.35\times\text{--}7.61\times$), whereas LOTO-CV yields $85.42\text{ ms}$ (vs.\ Polars $82.47\text{ ms}$).

\textbf{Conclusion:} AST-Route enables catalog-coupled routing with tunable latency--tail-risk control when training covers target SQL operators (\url{https://github.com/preecha-msu/bigquery-olap-benchmark}).

\section{Introduction}
Modern enterprise analytics increasingly operate over heterogeneous, polyglot data infrastructures spanning four distinct computing paradigms: elastic serverless cloud data warehouses such as Google BigQuery \cite{melnik2010dremel, melnik2020dremel, lounissi2025funda}, in-process C++ vectorized analytical database systems such as DuckDB \cite{raasveldt2019duckdb, kuiper2024duckdb_ooc}, Rust-based Apache Arrow streaming query engines such as Polars and Apache DataFusion \cite{lamb2024datafusion}, and SIMT GPU-accelerated relational engines \cite{chrysogelos2019hetexchange, shanbhag2020crystal}, alongside distributed multi-node systems like ClickHouse \cite{schulze2024clickhouse} and Trino \cite{sethi2019presto}. While cloud-native disaggregated storage-compute architectures provide massive horizontal slot concurrency for petabyte-scale workloads \cite{shojaee2024serverless, abdalla2022bigdata}, they introduce non-trivial stateless scheduling and distributed shuffle overheads on moderate-scale datasets \cite{begoli2018calcite, garcia2008database, lounissi2025funda}. Concurrently, modern local hardware presents an intricate architectural trade-off between CPU SIMD vectorization over Apache Arrow memory buffers and discrete GPU offloading across the PCIe interconnect \cite{milicevic2024dlquery, root2019mapd, kim2022blazingsql}.

Because each execution paradigm exhibits fundamentally different algorithmic strengths and memory-hierarchy bottlenecks across the discrete scale factors defined by the Transaction Processing Performance Council ($\text{SF} \in \{1, 10, 30, 100\text{ GB}\}$ in the TPC-H specification \cite{tpc2022tpch, boncz2014tpch, dreseler2020quantifying}), committing an entire analytical workload to any single static engine results in severe performance regret, excessive energy consumption, out-of-core spilling penalties, or unnecessary cloud FinOps expenditure. Designing an automated, pre-execution query routing framework capable of dynamically dispatching SQL queries to their optimal execution backend requires overcoming three critical research gaps in the existing literature \cite{ahmed2020hibench, oneil2009ssb, leis2015job}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Research Gap 1 (Limitations of Single-Engine Learned Optimizers and Homogeneous Cloud Routers):} Existing learned query optimizers such as Neo \cite{marcus2019neo} and Bao \cite{marcus2021bao} steer physical operator plans \emph{within} a single database engine, Zero-Shot Cost Models \cite{hilprecht2022zeroshot} require physical execution plan graphs, and stage-level cloud routers \cite{tan2023stage, gupta2023adaptive} assign jobs across homogeneous cloud clusters without modeling local Rust Arrow or discrete GPU hardware. A lightweight pre-execution framework that extracts static Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) features and zone-map selectivity directly from raw SQL to route queries across C++ DBMS, Rust Arrow, CUDA GPU, and serverless cloud paradigms remains underdeveloped.
    \item \textbf{Research Gap 2 (Unmodeled PCIe Crossover Thresholds, Memory Walls, and Device-Level Energy Costs):} Prior GPU database studies \cite{shanbhag2020crystal, root2019mapd, kim2022blazingsql} predominantly report warm in-VRAM kernel speedups at fixed small scale factors ($\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$) while omitting cold-start PCIe Host-to-Device (H2D) transfer overheads, chunked PCIe streaming when datasets exceed device VRAM ($\text{SF}=10, 30, \text{and } 100\text{ GB}$), out-of-core host RAM disk spilling \cite{kuiper2024duckdb_ooc}, and hardware energy efficiency ($\text{Joules}$ and $\text{MB/J}$).
    \item \textbf{Research Gap 3 (Confounded Complexity, Data Leakage, and Lack of Nested Cross-Workload Validation):} Traditional OLAP evaluations \cite{ahmed2020hibench, abdalla2022bigdata} rely on unstratified query sets where structural operator depth, storage layout (row-store vs. Parquet vs. Arrow), and predicate selectivity are confounded, and where random train-test splits across variations of the same query template induce severe data leakage without testing generalization to unseen seeds or nested cross-validation on standard TPC-H Q1--Q22 choke-point workloads \cite{boncz2014tpch, dreseler2020quantifying}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Bridging Empirical Crossover Profiling and Risk-Aware Multi-Objective Routing.}
Designing a reliable cross-paradigm dispatcher requires resolving a fundamental systems tension: static single-engine policies suffer severe performance cliffs across heterogeneous operators, yet naive point-estimate cost minimization ($\arg\min_e \hat{T}_e$) triggers costly tail-latency spikes whenever prediction intervals overlap near CPU--GPU PCIe crossover boundaries or device memory ceilings. Rather than treating hardware benchmarking and learned routing in isolation, this study grounds an interpretable, zero-execution routing architecture directly in controlled factorial crossover telemetry. Crucially, decoupling the pre-execution structural/volume representation ($\mathbf{x}_q \in \mathbb{R}^9$, extended to $\mathbf{x}_q^{\text{scale}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$) from the downstream decision policy exposes an explicit operational trade-off between \emph{unconstrained mean-latency minimization} (achievable via lightweight linear, shallow-tree, or $k$-NN estimators) and \emph{tail-safe SLA protection} (governed via calibrated conformal prediction intervals and resource-constrained Pareto dispatch). The primary contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{De-Confounded Factorial Testbed and CPU--GPU--Cloud Crossover Profiling:} We construct a 75-query AST-stratified factorial benchmark (25 canonical seeds $\times$ 3 selectivity horizons V1--V3 across tiers L1--L5, complemented by 22 official TPC-H, 33 IMDB JOB, 13 SSB, and 20 ClickBench queries; $N=163$ total) across six engines spanning row-store, serverless cloud, C++/Rust Apache Arrow vectorized CPUs, and discrete CUDA GPUs. By stripping SQL parsing/planning overhead ($183.74 - 0.87 = 182.87\text{ ms}$ pure DuckDB CPU kernel vs.\ $22.25\text{ ms}$ in-VRAM GPU kernel) and pairing direct NVML GPU power sensors with TDP-calibrated CPU package energy estimates and BigQuery $10\text{ MiB}$ minimum-floor scan billing, we quantify exact PCIe transfer crossovers and memory walls under 100\% real-execution ground-truth telemetry (zero synthetic latency simulation).

    \item \textbf{Construct-Validated AST Structural Complexity ($\text{CSCI}$) and Pre-Execution 9D/10D Representation:} We formulate a zero-execution 9-dimensional feature vector ($\mathbf{x}_q \in \mathbb{R}^9$, augmented with $\log_{10}(1+\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{scan}})$ to $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ across scales) extracted in $3.65\text{ ms}$ ($3.476\text{ ms}$ \texttt{SQLGlot} AST parsing plus $0.172\text{ ms}$ cached or $0.413\text{ ms}$ cold NVMe Parquet footer zone-map metadata; selectivity $\text{MAE}=3.84\%$, median Q-Error $=1.092$) without touching data pages. Extensive validation confirms construct correlation with AST node count ($\rho=0.8744$), partial correlation with GPU speedup controlling for selectivity ($\rho_{\text{partial}}=0.5477, p=3.68\times 10^{-7}$), 10,000-trial weight-perturbation rank stability ($\bar{\rho}=0.9880$ at $\pm 20\%$ and $\bar{\rho}=0.9555$ at $\pm 50\%$), and low tree-size multicollinearity ($\text{VIF}=1.84$ against $[D(q), N(q)]$). In axis-aligned tree ensembles, $\text{CSCI}(q)$ acts as an engineered oblique hyperplane projection that reduces active split nodes per tree by $-28.2\%$ and lowers out-of-fold routing regret by $-9.22\%$ ($p=0.0038$) over atomic AST counts alone.

    \item \textbf{Model-Agnostic Heterogeneous Cost Prediction, Standalone CPU-Only Routing, and Explicit Latency--Tail Risk Control:} Using $\mathbf{x}_q$, our multi-output cost predictors estimate cross-architecture latency, device energy, and peak working-set memory in $497.2\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ (median Q-Error $1.007\text{--}1.172$, $\log\text{-}R^2=0.815\text{--}0.912$). Under 25-seed LOSO-CV ($\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$), \emph{speed-prioritized point-estimate routers} using Ridge ($49.12\text{ ms}$), shallow decision trees ($51.35\text{ ms}$), $k$-NN ($52.84\text{ ms}$), or in-fold tuned rules ($53.07\text{ ms}$) minimize raw mean latency but incur high P95/P99 tail regret ($150.9\%\text{--}196.8\%$ / $268.4\%\text{--}342.1\%$), whereas our \emph{conformal risk-aware AST-Route} ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$ with 90\% conformal intervals, $\text{ECE}=1.78\%$, and a $12\%$ abstention guard) trades $3.6\text{--}5.8\text{ ms}$ of mean latency ($54.92\text{ ms}$ net warm, $55.16\text{ ms}$ cold footer, $57.33\%$ Top-1, $76.00\%$ Top-2) to compress P95/P99 tail regret to $114.11\%$/$218.40\%$ and halve $>100\%$ regret failures ($14.67\%\rightarrow 6.67\%$). Moreover, standalone CPU-only routing ($M_{\text{gpu\_max}}=0$) achieves $74.67\%$ Top-1 ($69.12\text{ ms}$) on the primary node and $86.67\%$ Top-1 ($62.05\text{ ms}$) on a 16-core workstation without GPU dependence.

    \item \textbf{Memory-Constrained Multi-Objective Pareto Dispatch (Latency, Energy, and Cloud Scan Charge):} Coupling $\hat{\sigma}(q)$ with catalog byte footprints ($\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{scan}}(q)=\hat{\sigma}(q)\cdot\mathcal{B}_{\text{catalog}}$) enforces strict VRAM/DRAM feasibility filtering (eliminating 100\% of out-of-memory failures under tight VRAM caps) while jointly optimizing execution latency, estimated active device energy ($1.381\text{ J/query}$), and marginal on-demand Cloud Query Scan Charges ($\text{\$6.25/TiB}$, cleanly separated from sunk local hardware amortization). Under independent seed-level ($N=25$) Holm--Bonferroni inference, AST-Route significantly outperforms static CPU engines ($1.50\times\text{--}3.35\times, p_{\text{Holm}}\le 0.0171$) and static rule-based routing ($p=0.0187$), while matching Always GPU in overall mean latency ($54.92$ vs.\ $56.96\text{ ms}, p=0.184$) at less than half the P95/P99 tail regret.

    \item \textbf{Multi-Scale, Concurrent, Cross-Hardware, and Operator-Bounded Generalization:} Across $\text{SF}\in\{1, 10, 30, 100\}\text{ GB}$ ($6.0\text{M}\text{--}600.1\text{M}$ rows), $C=1\text{--}32$ concurrent query streams, and secondary multi-core CPU-only hardware profiles, AST-Route sustains $3.35\times\text{--}7.61\times$ speedups over DuckDB and $2.00\times\text{--}2.14\times$ steady-state throughput gains (up to $154.44\text{ QPS}$). It transfers effectively to locked unseen seeds ($73.33\%$ Top-1, $47.01\text{ ms}$), official TPC-H Q1--Q22 ($68.18\%$ Top-1, $90.91\%$ Top-2, $16.98\text{ ms}$), zero-shot star-join SSB ($92.31\%$ Top-1, $12.15\text{ ms}$), wide-table ClickBench web analytics ($80.00\%$ Top-1, $95.00\%$ Top-2, $24.18\text{ ms}$), and string-skewed cyclic JOB queries ($6.75\text{ ms}$ Polars parity via $21.21\%$ conformal abstention), while Leave-One-Tier-Out evaluation (LOTO-CV: $85.42\text{ ms}$ vs.\ Polars $82.47\text{ ms}$) transparently establishes that training workloads must span target SQL operator families.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work and Architectural Representativeness}
\subsection{Cloud MPP Warehouses, Distributed Query Engines, and Vectorized CPU Systems}
Disaggregated cloud data warehouses pioneered by Google Dremel/BigQuery \cite{melnik2010dremel, melnik2020dremel} and distributed MPP query engines such as Presto/Trino \cite{sethi2019presto}, ClickHouse \cite{schulze2024clickhouse}, and Apache Spark \cite{ahmed2020hibench} decouple compute workers from durable storage. Recent studies in serverless data pipelines \cite{shojaee2024serverless} and in-situ serverless query processing such as FunDa \cite{lounissi2025funda} highlight the fundamental tension between cold-start scheduling overhead and horizontal slot elasticity. Specifically, while Trino \cite{sethi2019presto} excels at federated cross-source query execution over distributed object stores and ClickHouse \cite{schulze2024clickhouse} achieves high throughput via shared-nothing merge-tree storage and SIMD vectorization across cluster nodes, both introduce network serialization and coordinator scheduling overheads when deployed on single-node or moderate-scale datasets. Conversely, embeddable C++ vectorized engines such as DuckDB \cite{raasveldt2019duckdb, kuiper2024duckdb_ooc} process columnar Row Groups in-process using morsel-driven parallelism and out-of-core external memory management, whereas next-generation Rust engines (Polars and Apache DataFusion \cite{lamb2024datafusion}) execute zero-copy SIMD and streaming pipelines directly over standardized Apache Arrow memory buffers.

\subsection{GPU-Accelerated Databases, Learned Query Optimizers, and Adaptive Query Routing}
In the domain of GPU-accelerated relational analytics, systems such as HeavyDB (formerly OmniSci/MapD) \cite{root2019mapd}, BlazingSQL over RAPIDS cuDF \cite{kim2022blazingsql}, Crystal \cite{shanbhag2020crystal}, and HetExchange \cite{chrysogelos2019hetexchange} leverage massive SIMT parallelism and high-bandwidth device memory (VRAM) to accelerate relational joins and group-by aggregations. However, as demonstrated by Chrysogelos et al. \cite{chrysogelos2019hetexchange} and Shanbhag et al. \cite{shanbhag2020crystal}, discrete GPU execution is fundamentally constrained by the PCIe interconnect when data resides in host DRAM or exceeds device VRAM capacity, necessitating rigorous separation of PCIe transfer latency from kernel compute time.

Concurrently, recent surveys on deep learning in query execution \cite{milicevic2024dlquery} and learned query optimization have advanced along three distinct trajectories: (i) \emph{Intra-DBMS Plan Steering} systems such as Neo \cite{marcus2019neo} and Bao \cite{marcus2021bao}, which use tree-convolutional neural networks to generate end-to-end physical execution plans (Neo) or select contextual-bandit optimizer hint sets via Thompson sampling (Bao) inside a single relational engine (e.g., PostgreSQL) but cannot route queries across disparate execution paradigms; (ii) \emph{Learned Physical Cost Models} such as Zero-Shot Cost Models \cite{hilprecht2022zeroshot}, which generalize cost prediction across unseen single-node databases, yet still require compiled physical operator plan graphs and exact cardinalities rather than pre-execution raw SQL syntax; and (iii) \emph{Cloud Stage Routers} such as polystore and adaptive cloud routers \cite{tan2023stage, gupta2023adaptive}, which route query stages across homogeneous cloud engines based on runtime history without modeling local Rust Arrow streaming, discrete GPU PCIe crossover thresholds ($\Phi_{\text{PCIe}}$), or hardware energy efficiency ($\eta_{\text{watt}}$). In parallel, the TPC-H decision support benchmark specification \cite{tpc2022tpch} establishes discrete scale factors ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30, 100\text{ GB}$) and 22 canonical queries (Q1--Q22) whose internal execution choke points (multi-way join graphs in Q2, Q5, Q7, Q8, Q9, Q21 and correlated subqueries in Q16--Q22) have been formalized by Boncz et al. \cite{boncz2014tpch} and Dreseler et al. \cite{dreseler2020quantifying}. Complementing classical OLAP benchmarks such as SSB \cite{oneil2009ssb} and JOB \cite{leis2015job}, modern text-to-SQL benchmarks such as BIRD \cite{li2023bird} and Spider 2.0 \cite{lei2025spider2} incorporate the Valid Efficiency Score (VES), while AST transpilers such as \texttt{SQLGlot} \cite{mao2024sqlglot} and Apache Calcite \cite{begoli2018calcite} enable deterministic structural analysis. Most recently, modular heterogeneous execution engines such as Maximus [33] and DuckDB-integrated GPU accelerators such as Sirius [34] have demonstrated substantial SIMT speedups when relational working sets fit within device HBM/VRAM, while energy-aware and risk-aware learned query optimization frameworks such as E-LQO [35] and Roq [36] have highlighted the importance of energy telemetry and prediction uncertainty within single-engine optimizers. \subsection{Rationale for Target Execution Engines in the AST-Route Framework}
Rather than exhaustively evaluating every commercial DBMS, \textbf{AST-Route} targets six software engines that serve as canonical, non-redundant representatives of the four foundational execution paradigms in modern data engineering:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{SQLite 3 (Canonical Tuple-at-a-Time B-Tree Row-Store Baseline):} Represents traditional ANSI C row-oriented storage using a Volcano-style iterator model, establishing the exact performance and energy baseline of B-tree page traversal without columnar compression or SIMD vectorization.
    \item \textbf{Google BigQuery (Canonical Serverless Disaggregated Cloud OLAP):} Represents petabyte-scale cloud-native execution (Dremel/Capacitor columnar storage with dynamic slot allocation) \cite{melnik2020dremel}, isolating distributed cloud orchestration overhead and storage clustering FinOps economics without local hardware bottlenecks.
    \item \textbf{DuckDB (Canonical C++ Morsel-Driven Columnar DBMS):} Represents state-of-the-art in-process C++ analytical database design \cite{raasveldt2019duckdb, kuiper2024duckdb_ooc} with tightly integrated storage block management, parallel segment-tree window framing, and out-of-core external disk spilling.
    \item \textbf{Polars \& Apache DataFusion (Canonical Rust Apache Arrow Engines):} Represent the emerging Rust Arrow ecosystem via two distinct optimizer philosophies: \emph{Polars} exemplifies a dataframe-native lazy streaming engine over Arrow arrays, whereas \emph{Apache DataFusion} \cite{lamb2024datafusion} exemplifies a modular SQL physical query planner (used as the core execution engine in modern distributed systems such as InfluxDB 3.0 and Ballista).
    \item \textbf{PyTorch CUDA Relational Tensor Engine (Canonical SIMT GPU Execution):} Represents hardware-timed GPU relational tensor processing over contiguous device buffers (analogous to cuDF/BlazingSQL \cite{kim2022blazingsql} and Crystal \cite{shanbhag2020crystal}), enabling exact hardware-counter decomposition of PCIe H2D transfers, chunked out-of-VRAM streaming, and in-VRAM SIMT kernel execution on consumer/edge GPU hardware (NVIDIA RTX 2050).
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Proposed AST-Route Framework Architecture}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:proposed_ast_framework} and the end-to-end validation workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:experimental_workflow}, \textbf{AST-Route} is structured as a modular four-layer architecture that decouples static query representation from multi-paradigm hardware cost modeling and automated engine dispatching.

\subsection{Layer 1: Static AST Structural Profiling, Five-Tier Workload, and Official TPC-H Scale Tiers ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30, 100\text{ GB}$)}
Given an incoming SQL query $q \in \mathcal{Q}$, Layer 1 parses the SQL statement into a canonical Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) using \texttt{SQLGlot} \cite{mao2024sqlglot} without touching underlying data pages. Traversing the AST yields seven structural operator dimensions: tree depth $D(q)$, total AST operator nodes $N(q)$, relational join count $J(q)$, nested CTE/subquery depth $S(q)$, window frame specifications $W(q)$, repeated field unnesting operators $U(q)$, and conditional \texttt{CASE} branches $C(q)$. While total AST node count $N(q)$ captures overall query verbosity (and is retained directly as the second coordinate in our 9D routing vector $\mathbf{x}_q$), raw node counts are dominated by scalar expressions and projection lists that exhibit high collinearity with tree depth. To isolate the non-linear algorithmic complexity of relational operators without expression-length inflation, we formulate the Composite Structural Complexity Index ($\text{CSCI}$) over the six core structural operators:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CSCI}(q) = 1.5 D(q) + 2.5 J(q) + 3.0 S(q) + 3.5 W(q) + 4.0 U(q) + 1.5 C(q)
\end{equation}
In accordance with the official TPC-H benchmark specification Clause 4.1.3 \cite{tpc2022tpch} and established multi-scale choke-point methodologies \cite{boncz2014tpch, dreseler2020quantifying, kuiper2024duckdb_ooc}, we provision the 8-table TPC-H relational schema across four standard discrete scale factors designed to systematically stress every layer of the modern memory hierarchy:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{$\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$ (In-VRAM \& In-DRAM Baseline Regime):} Contains $6.00\times 10^6$ \texttt{lineitem} rows ($8.66\times 10^6$ total rows across 8 tables). The working set fits entirely within both the $4.0\text{ GB}$ discrete GPU VRAM buffer ($1.30\text{ GB}$ peak) and the $16.0\text{ GB}$ host DRAM ($4.71\text{ GB}$ peak) with zero disk spilling.
    \item \textbf{$\text{SF}=10\text{ GB}$ (Out-of-VRAM GPU Streaming \& In-DRAM Host Regime):} Contains $60.0\times 10^6$ \texttt{lineitem} rows ($86.6\times 10^6$ total rows). Uncompressed working projections exceed the $4.0\text{ GB}$ GPU VRAM ceiling, forcing 4-chunk pipelined PCIe H2D streaming ($3.45\text{ GB}$ peak VRAM) while remaining primarily in host DRAM ($7.85\text{ GB}$ peak RAM, $125.0\text{ MB}$ minor spill).
    \item \textbf{$\text{SF}=30\text{ GB}$ (Host DRAM Cap Inflection \& Moderate Out-of-Core Spill Regime):} Contains $180.0\times 10^6$ \texttt{lineitem} rows ($259.8\times 10^6$ total rows) \cite{tpc2022tpch}. Intermediate hash-join and window-sort partitions exceed the configured $9.00\text{ GB}$ host DBMS buffer pool cap, triggering active out-of-core disk spilling \cite{kuiper2024duckdb_ooc} ($864.0\text{ MB}$ mean spill) and 12-chunk GPU PCIe streaming ($3.68\text{ GB}$ peak VRAM).
    \item \textbf{$\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$ (Extreme Out-of-Core Spilling \& Cloud Elasticity Crossover Regime):} Contains $600.1\times 10^6$ \texttt{lineitem} rows ($866.1\times 10^6$ total rows) \cite{tpc2022tpch, dreseler2020quantifying}, inducing heavy external disk spilling on local CPU engines ($3302.0\text{ MB}$ mean spill, peaking at $6840.0\text{ MB}$ in L2 joins) and 40-chunk PCIe transfers on the GPU ($3.85\text{ GB}$ peak VRAM).
\end{itemize}

Across this schema, we evaluate two complementary SQL workload suites: (A) our 75-query \emph{TPC-H-derived, AST-stratified synthetic analytical workload} instantiated from 25 canonical seeds across five modern structural complexity tiers ($5 \times 5 = 25$ independent seeds, mean $\text{CSCI} = 29.66$), and (B) the 22 \emph{official standard TPC-H benchmark queries} (\textbf{Q1--Q22}, mean $\text{CSCI} = 26.52$, range $12.0\text{--}47.5$) \cite{tpc2022tpch, boncz2014tpch}. Because the base TPC-H schema consists of flat relational tables, Tier L5 dynamically constructs nested array columns in-query via staged \texttt{ARRAY\_AGG} / \texttt{LIST} aggregation over one-to-many \texttt{orders}--\texttt{lineitem} relationships before benchmarking lateral \texttt{CROSS JOIN UNNEST} re-aggregation:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{L1 (Scan, SARGability, \& Monotonic Conditional Branching):} Single-table columnar scans with multi-branch \texttt{CASE WHEN} expressions and distinct cardinality aggregations ($D = 9.0, J = 0.0, \text{CSCI} = 15.3$).
    \item \textbf{L2 (Multi-Way Star, Snowflake, \& Bilateral Joins):} 4-way to 8-way relational joins, composite \texttt{partsupp} joins, and non-equi late-delivery predicates ($D = 9.2, J = 4.8, \text{CSCI} = 26.8$).
    \item \textbf{L3 (Multi-Stage CTE Pipelines, Null-Safe Anti-Joins, \& One-Grain-Per-CTE):} 3-stage to 4-stage Common Table Expressions enforcing pre-aggregated grain isolation and null-safe anti-joins ($D = 10.8, J = 2.6, \text{CSCI} = 36.7$).
    \item \textbf{L4 (Multi-Axis Window Ranking, Lead/Lag, \& Sliding Frames):} Concurrent window partitions (\texttt{RANK}, \texttt{DENSE\_RANK}, \texttt{NTILE(10)}, \texttt{LAG}, \texttt{LEAD}, and bounded \texttt{ROWS BETWEEN} sliding windows; $D = 10.8, J = 1.6, \text{CSCI} = 37.1$).
    \item \textbf{L5 (Semi-Structured Array Construction \& Lateral UNNEST):} Multi-stage \texttt{ARRAY\_AGG} vector construction followed by lateral \texttt{CROSS JOIN UNNEST} re-aggregation ($D = 10.6, J = 2.2, \text{CSCI} = 32.4$).
\end{enumerate}

To capture data-volume sensitivity alongside syntax topology without executing the query, each of the 25 canonical seeds is instantiated across three selectivity perturbation horizons ($25 \times 3 = 75$ queries): V1 (High Selectivity, $\sim 1\text{--}5\%$ data), V2 (Medium Selectivity, $\sim 15\text{--}30\%$ data), and V3 (Adversarial Full-Horizon, $\sim 65\text{--}95\%$ data). As detailed in Table 2B, Layer 1 extracts the pre-execution predicate selectivity estimate $\hat{\sigma}(q) \in (0, 1]$ in $172.0\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ from a pre-cached metadata snapshot ($412.6\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ cold footer read on Apache Parquet; $845.2\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ live \texttt{PRAGMA storage\_info} query on DuckDB) strictly prior to query execution without scanning data blocks or touching post-execution results. Crucially, we distinguish the exact metadata capabilities and limitations of each storage engine: (i) \emph{Parquet and DuckDB Row-Group Zone Maps}: \texttt{pyarrow.parquet.ParquetFile.metadata} and DuckDB \texttt{PRAGMA storage\_info('table')} expose column-level minimum, maximum, and null-count statistics across $100\%$ of numeric and date columns (e.g., \texttt{l\_shipdate} $\in [\texttt{1992-01-02}, \texttt{1998-12-01}]$, \texttt{l\_quantity} $\in [1.0, 50.0]$), enabling accurate range interpolation on both partitioned and non-partitioned numeric predicates (comparing estimated $\hat{\sigma}(q)$ against true post-execution row selectivity $\sigma_{\text{actual}}(q) = |R_{\text{out}}|/|R_{\text{table}}|$ yields a mean absolute error of $\text{MAE}=3.84\%$ and median Q-Error $=1.092$ across the 75-query suite, and $\text{MAE}=1.52\%$, Q-Error $=1.263$ on non-partitioned multi-attribute numeric predicates); (ii) \emph{BigQuery Partition Pruning Statistics}: \texttt{INFORMATION\_SCHEMA.PARTITIONS} and dry-run byte estimation provide \emph{partition pruning and clustering statistics} (\texttt{partition\_id}, \texttt{total\_rows}, \texttt{total\_logical\_bytes}) rather than general column-level min/max zone maps for every attribute. When predicates filter on the partition key (\texttt{l\_shipdate}), partition pruning metadata estimates selectivity within $\text{MAE}=4.26\%$; however, on non-partitioned columns where BigQuery does not expose column-level min/max bounds, AST-Route either references the shared Parquet footer catalog or falls back to parametric AST operator priors (which we explicitly evaluate in Table 9 Exp C by masking $50\%$ of metadata, sustaining $53.33\%$ Top-1 accuracy and $68.42\text{ ms}$ mean latency); and (iii) \emph{String Predicates and Skewed Distributions}: because continuous min/max zone maps cannot bound unanchored substring filters (\texttt{LIKE '\%brass\%'}) or capture intra-range Zipfian skew ($s=1.2$), estimation error increases to $\text{MAE}=5.00\%$ (Q-Error $=2.000$) on substring predicates and $\text{MAE}=38.8\%$ (Q-Error $=4.880$) under severe power-law skew (explaining the $21.21\%$ Top-1 accuracy on string-skewed IMDB JOB queries in Table 11 Part B). Combining the seven static AST operator counts with $\text{CSCI}(q)$ and estimated selectivity $\hat{\sigma}(q)$ yields the 9-dimensional routing feature vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_q = \big[\, D(q),\; N(q),\; J(q),\; S(q),\; W(q),\; U(q),\; C(q),\; \text{CSCI}(q),\; \sigma(q) \,\big]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{9}
\end{equation}
 Crucially, while $\mathbf{x}_q \in \mathbb{R}^9$ encodes scale-invariant syntax topology and relative selectivity $\hat{\sigma}(q) \in (0,1]$, multi-scale awareness across $\text{SF} \in \{1, 10, 30, 100\}$ GB is achieved in Layers 2--4 by multiplying $\hat{\sigma}(q)$ with the pre-execution catalog table byte footprint $\mathcal{B}_{\text{catalog}}$ ($\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{scan}}(q) = \hat{\sigma}(q)\cdot \mathcal{B}_{\text{catalog}}$) inside the analytical memory/PCIe chunk estimator and the capacity filter $\mathcal{E}_{\text{feasible}}(q)$ alongside scale-calibrated cost predictors.

\subsection{Layer 2: Cross-Paradigm Analytical Cost, PCIe Crossover, and Device Energy Telemetry Formulation}
To enable apples-to-apples cost evaluation across serverless cloud, local CPU, and discrete GPU backends, Layer 2 establishes six analytical cost and hardware telemetry models:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{CUDA Cold-Start Decomposition and PCIe Bottleneck Fraction ($\Phi_{\text{PCIe}}$):} On discrete GPUs (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050, $4.0\text{ GB}$ VRAM), cold-start execution time $T_{\text{GPU\_Cold}}(q)$ is decomposed into PCIe Host-to-Device transfer time ($T_{\text{PCIe\_H2D}}$) and pure in-VRAM kernel compute time ($T_{\text{GPU\_Warm}}$):
    \begin{equation}
        T_{\text{GPU\_Cold}}(q) = T_{\text{PCIe\_H2D}}(q) + T_{\text{GPU\_Warm}}(q), \qquad \Phi_{\text{PCIe}}(q) = \frac{T_{\text{PCIe\_H2D}}(q)}{T_{\text{GPU\_Cold}}(q)} \times 100\%
    \end{equation}
    Cold-start GPU offloading outperforms vectorized CPU execution ($T_{\text{GPU\_Cold}}(q) < T_{\text{CPU}}(q)$) if and only if the warm kernel speedup satisfies the break-even condition $\text{Speedup}_{\text{Warm}}(q) > (1 - \Phi_{\text{PCIe}}(q)/100)^{-1}$.
    \item \textbf{Effective Analytical Scan Throughput ($\Theta_{\text{eff}}$):} Normalizing unpruned columnar volume $\mathcal{B}_{\text{unopt}}(q)$ (in MB) against execution time $T_{e}(q)$ (in ms) yields effective processing throughput in $\text{GB/s}$:
    \begin{equation}
        \Theta_{\text{eff}}(q, e) = \frac{\mathcal{B}_{\text{unopt}}(q)}{1.024 \cdot T_{e}(q)} \quad [\text{GB/s}]
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Cloud Slot-to-Wall-Time Concurrency Proxy ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{slot}}$) and FinOps Cost Savings ($\Delta C_{\text{1M}}$):} Quantifies cumulative distributed slot compute time relative to server wall-clock time on BigQuery (serving as an effective concurrency proxy), alongside financial savings per $10^6$ queries under on-demand bytes-scanned pricing ($\alpha_{\text{TB}} = \$6.25\text{ USD/TiB}$):
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{M}_{\text{slot}}(q) = \frac{T_{\text{BQ\_Slot}}(q)}{T_{\text{BQ\_Wall}}(q)}, \qquad \Delta C_{\text{1M}}(q) = 10^6 \times \left( \frac{\mathcal{B}_{\text{unopt}}(q) - \mathcal{B}_{\text{clustered}}(q)}{1024^2} \right) \times \alpha_{\text{TB}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Active Device Energy Telemetry ($E(q,e)$), 50-Repetition Bland--Altman Calibration, Three-Scope Boundary Separation, and Energy-Aware Routing Scope ($\eta_{\text{watt}}$):} High-frequency telemetry polling (200--250 Hz via \texttt{psutil}, NVIDIA NVML \texttt{nvmlDeviceGetPowerUsage}, and \texttt{torch.cuda} counters across $N=5$ benchmark trials per query and $R=50$ calibration repetitions per workload tier, totaling $250$ calibration runs; measurement $\text{CV} = 2.84\%\text{--}3.52\%$) records active CPU utilization ($\text{CPU}_{\text{util}}\%$), peak host RAM ($\text{RAM}_{\text{used}}$ in GB), disk spilling ($\text{Spill}_{\text{disk}}$ in MB), GPU SM utilization ($\text{GPU}_{\text{util}}\%$), and peak device memory ($\text{VRAM}_{\text{used}}$ in GB). To maintain strict physical transparency across hardware interfaces, we separate energy metrics into three explicit boundary scopes and refrain from claiming whole-system wall-socket energy savings: (i) \emph{Measured GPU Board Energy ($E_{\text{GPU}}$)}: sampled directly from physical NVIDIA NVML on-board hardware power sensors ($10.5\text{ W}$ idle baseline; 50-repetition $\text{MAE} = 0.008\text{ J}$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.010\text{ J}$, relative error $\text{MACE} = 1.24\%$, Bland--Altman bias $+0.001\text{ J}$ with 95\% limits of agreement [LoA]: $[-0.018, +0.020]\text{ J}$); (ii) \emph{Estimated CPU Package Energy ($E_{\text{CPU}}$)}: because unprivileged ring-3 processes on Windows 11 cannot read ring-0 Intel RAPL / AMD SMU MSR energy registers directly without a kernel driver, active CPU package power is modeled via TDP-calibrated utilization polling $P_{\text{CPU}}(t) = P_{\text{idle}} + (P_{\text{TDP}} - P_{\text{idle}})\cdot(\text{CPU}_{\text{util}}(t)/100)$ ($14.5\text{ W}$ idle, $45.0\text{ W}$ TDP). Cross-validating this linear TDP-calibrated estimate against a non-linear CMOS DVFS voltage-frequency reference curve ($P \propto u^{1.28}$) across $R=50$ repetitions per workload tier yields an absolute error of $\text{MAE} = 0.205\text{ J}$ ($\text{RMSE} = 0.214\text{ J}$) and Bland--Altman bias of $-0.004\text{ J}$ (95\% LoA: $[-0.422, +0.414]\text{ J}$) on DuckDB ($6.40\text{ J}$ mean), and $\text{MAE} = 0.098\text{ J}$ ($\text{RMSE} = 0.102\text{ J}$, Bland--Altman bias $-0.002\text{ J}$, 95\% LoA: $[-0.201, +0.197]\text{ J}$) on Polars ($3.17\text{ J}$ mean), corresponding to a mean relative calibration deviation of $\text{MACE} = 3.09\%\text{--}3.21\%$ and rank concordance $\rho \ge 0.9982$ (we emphasize that this $3.09\%\text{--}3.21\%$ MACE bounds analytical curve-form sensitivity between linear TDP interpolation and non-linear DVFS scaling rather than certifying accuracy within $3.21\%$ of physical ring-0 RAPL/SMU hardware counters, which we complement macroscopically via 45-second ACPI DC battery-shunt discharge telemetry); and (iii) \emph{Composite Host+Device System Energy ($E_{\text{sys}} = E_{\text{GPU}} + E_{\text{CPU\_assist}}$)}: combines measured GPU board energy with estimated concurrent host CPU DMA/driver energy ($16.17\text{--}16.33\text{ W}$) during PCIe transfers ($1.58\text{ J}$ cold, $0.61\text{ J}$ warm; $\text{MAE} = 0.031\text{ J}$, $\text{MACE} = 1.96\%$, 95\% LoA: $[-0.064, +0.062]\text{ J}$). Because $E_{\text{CPU}}$ is an utilization-based package estimate rather than a ring-0 RAPL counter reading and excludes AC-DC PSU conversion, DRAM DIMM, and NVMe SSD rails (which require an external inline AC power meter), we explicitly designate CPU energy figures as \emph{Estimated CPU Package Energy} and frame our multi-objective dispatcher as \emph{energy-aware routing} rather than claiming physical whole-system energy certification. Integrating $\bar{P}_e(q)$ over execution duration $T_e(q)$ yields device energy in Joules and energy efficiency in $\text{MB/s/W} \equiv \text{MB/J}$:
\begin{equation}
        E(q, e) = \int_{t_0}^{t_0 + T_e(q)} P_e(t)\,dt \approx \bar{P}_e(q) \cdot \left(\frac{T_e(q)}{1000}\right) \;\; [\text{Joules}], \qquad \eta_{\text{watt}}(q, e) = \frac{\mathcal{B}_{\text{unopt}}(q)}{E(q, e)} \;\; [\text{MB/J}]
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Layers 3 \& 4: Multi-Output Cost-Regression Predictor, Q-Error Metric, and Constrained Multi-Objective Dispatcher}
In Layer 3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:proposed_ast_framework}), \textbf{AST-Route} employs a Multi-Output Gradient Boosting Regressor $f_{\Theta}: \mathbb{R}^{9} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{M}$ to simultaneously predict log-transformed execution latency $\widehat{\ln(1 + T_e)}(\mathbf{x}_q)$, energy footprint $\hat{E}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)$, and memory consumption ($\widehat{\text{RAM}}_e, \widehat{\text{VRAM}}_e$) across the candidate execution engines $\mathcal{E} = \{\text{DuckDB}, \text{Polars}, \text{DataFusion}, \text{GPU}_{\text{Cold}}, \text{BigQuery}\}$. Predictive accuracy of the cost model is evaluated via out-of-fold log-$R^2$, Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and the multiplicative \textbf{Q-Error} metric standard in database query optimizer research \cite{leis2015job, hilprecht2022zeroshot}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Q-Error}(y, \hat{y}) = \max\!\left( \frac{\hat{y}}{y},\; \frac{y}{\hat{y}} \right) \ge 1.0
\end{equation}
In Layer 4, the dispatcher selects the optimal engine minimizing a memory-constrained, multi-objective Pareto cost function parameterized by user-configurable weights $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, 1]$ ($\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$, where $\alpha$ weights normalized latency $\hat{T}_e/T_{\text{med}}$, $\beta$ weights normalized energy $\hat{E}_e/E_{\text{med}}$, and $\gamma$ weights normalized marginal cloud scan charge $\hat{C}_e/C_{\text{ref}}$) subject to hardware memory capacity budgets ($\widehat{\text{RAM}}_e(\mathbf{x}_q) \le M_{\text{host\_max}}$ and $\widehat{\text{VRAM}}_e(\mathbf{x}_q) \le M_{\text{gpu\_max}}$). Let $e^*(q) = \arg\min_{e \in \mathcal{E}} T_e(q)$ denote the ground-truth Oracle optimal engine:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{e}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(q) = \arg\min_{e \in \mathcal{E}_{\text{feasible}}(q)} \left[ \alpha \cdot \frac{\hat{T}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)}{T_{\text{med}}(q)} + \beta \cdot \frac{\hat{E}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)}{E_{\text{med}}(q)} + \gamma \cdot \frac{\hat{C}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)}{C_{\text{ref}}(q)} \right], \qquad \mathcal{R}_{\text{regret}}(q) = \frac{T_{\hat{e}(q)}(q) - T_{e^*(q)}(q)}{T_{e^*(q)}(q)} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}_{\text{feasible}}(q) = \{e \in \mathcal{E} \mid \widehat{\text{RAM}}_e(\mathbf{x}_q) \le M_{\text{host\_max}} \land \widehat{\text{VRAM}}_e(\mathbf{x}_q) \le M_{\text{gpu\_max}}\}$ prunes any candidate engine whose predicted memory footprint violates hardware capacity ceilings prior to objective scoring, and each objective term is normalized by its cross-engine reference scale ($T_{\text{med}}(q)$, $E_{\text{med}}(q)$, and $C_{\text{ref}}(q) = \max(C_{\text{BQ}}(q), \epsilon)$) to make heterogeneous units ($\text{ms}$, $\text{Joules}$, and $\text{USD}$) dimensionless and directly summable. To maintain strict accounting clarity, we decompose total operational expenditure into: (i) incremental \emph{On-Demand Cloud Query Scan Charge} $\hat{C}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)$ ($\$0.00$ marginal cloud billing fee for local CPU/GPU execution vs.\ $(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{clustered}}(\mathbf{x}_q)/2^{40})\times\$6.25/\text{TiB}$ on BigQuery), (ii) local active device energy consumption $\hat{E}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)$ (which corresponds to $\$0.02\text{--}\$0.21$ per $10^6$ queries at $\$0.12/\text{kWh}$ and is governed directly by the $\beta$-weighted energy term in Eq.~(8)), and (iii) sunk local hardware capital amortization ($\approx\$0.58\text{--}\$1.94$ per $10^6$ queries over a 3-year workstation lifecycle at $\$0.038/\text{hr}$). Because local hardware amortization is a fixed sunk cost independent of per-query routing, $\hat{C}_e(\mathbf{x}_q)$ in Eq.~(8) specifically represents the marginal \emph{Cloud Query Scan Charge} (whose operational footprint is captured by energy $\hat{E}_e$) and $(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\text{clustered}}(\mathbf{x}_q)/2^{40})\times \$6.25\text{/TiB}$ for BigQuery, while working-set memory operates as a hard pre-dispatch feasibility filter $\mathcal{E}_{\text{feasible}}(q)$ under pre-specified SLA weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$. Furthermore, under confidence-aware routing, if the predicted latency margin between a discrete GPU candidate and the top vectorized CPU candidate is within a narrow uncertainty band ($|\hat{T}_{\text{CPU}} - \hat{T}_{\text{GPU}}|/\hat{T}_{\text{GPU}} < 12\%$) on simple scan queries ($J(q)=0$), the dispatcher abstains from PCIe transfer risk and selects the deterministic in-memory CPU engine.

To rigorously prevent data leakage across query variations sharing the same template structure, we evaluate \textbf{AST-Route} under three complementary validation protocols: (i) \textbf{25-fold Leave-One-Seed-Out cross-validation (LOSO-CV)} across all 25 disjoint seed groups ($N=75$ queries), (ii) a \textbf{Strict Unseen Seed Holdout Split}, where the framework is trained exclusively on 20 development seeds (\texttt{S1}--\texttt{S4} across L1--L5, $N_{\text{dev}}=60$ queries) and evaluated once on 5 completely locked unseen test seeds (\texttt{L1\_S5, L2\_S5, L3\_S5, L4\_S5, L5\_S5}, $N_{\text{unseen}}=15$ queries), and (iii) \textbf{Strict Nested 22-fold Leave-One-Query-Out cross-validation (Nested LOO-CV)} on the 22 official standard TPC-H benchmark queries (\textbf{Q1--Q22}) \cite{tpc2022tpch}, where feature calibration weights are tuned strictly inside the 21-query inner training loop without touching the held-out test query. We benchmark \textbf{AST-Route} ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{RF-9D}}$ and $\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$) against four baseline policies: \textbf{(1) Always DuckDB}, \textbf{(2) Always GPU (Cold-Start)}, \textbf{(3) Always BigQuery (Cloud OLAP)}, and \textbf{(4) Rule-Based Routing} across Execution Time ($T_{\pi}$), Routing Accuracy ($\text{Acc}_{\text{top1}}$, $\text{Acc}_{\text{top2}}$, $\le 10\%$ near-optimal hit rate, and zero-regret hit rate), Resource Cost, and Normalized Performance Regret ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{regret}}$, including Mean, Median P50, P90, and P95 Tail Regret).

\section{Experimental Validation and Empirical Analysis}
\subsection{In-Process Storage Effect: External Parquet vs. Native Columnar}
Table~\ref{tab:empirical_complexity_results} summarizes the local execution telemetry on DuckDB across all 75 queries ($N=375$ trial executions). Overall, native columnar storage reduces mean latency from $254.42\text{ ms}$ (seed-clustered 95\% CI: $[163.65, 362.54]\text{ ms}$) to $183.74\text{ ms}$ (95\% CI: $[110.71, 274.89]\text{ ms}$). Across L1 scans ($81.60\text{ ms} \rightarrow 50.84\text{ ms}$, $1.60\times$), L2 multi-way joins ($403.94\text{ ms} \rightarrow 264.53\text{ ms}$), L3 staged CTE pipelines ($232.34\text{ ms} \rightarrow 121.92\text{ ms}$, $1.91\times$), and L4 window functions ($111.52\text{ ms} \rightarrow 78.16\text{ ms}$), native columnar storage consistently outperforms external Parquet decoding ($p = 1.542 \times 10^{-12}$ across queries, $p = 1.783 \times 10^{-55}$ across trials; Cliff's delta $\delta = +0.296\text{ [Small]}$) by eliminating repeated ZSTD decompression overhead.



\subsection{Cloud Serverless vs. Vectorized CPU, Slot Elasticity, and Multi-Scale FinOps Savings (V1--V3)}
Tables~\ref{tab:cross_engine_main}, \ref{tab:selectivity_sensitivity}, and \ref{tab:throughput_and_cost}, together with Figs.~\ref{fig:cross_engine_results} and \ref{fig:seed25_heatmap}, demonstrate that while DuckDB executes L1--L5 queries at $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$ in $50.8\text{--}403.3\text{ ms}$ (mean $183.74\text{ ms}$), Google BigQuery exhibits a serverless scheduling floor of $372.8\text{--}1761.0\text{ ms}$ server wall-clock time (mean $745.39\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[515.64, 1026.49]\text{ ms}$, reduced from $1412.6\text{ ms}$ server / $1412.60\text{ ms}$ client-observed unoptimized latency, 95\% CI: $[982.40, 1914.50]\text{ ms}$; Cliff's $\delta = +0.471\text{ [Medium]}, p = 6.89 \times 10^{-13}$) while dynamically allocating $4491.3\text{ ms}$ of parallel slot compute time ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{slot}} = 6.37\times$, peaking at $9.41\times$ in L3). Under V1 high-selectivity predicates, BigQuery clustering keys reduce mean scanned bytes by $\mathbf{78.4\%}$ ($347.7\text{ MB} \rightarrow 75.1\text{ MB}$ at $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$). As shown in Table~\ref{tab:selectivity_sensitivity}, this $78.4\%$ physical block pruning translates into FinOps cost reductions ($\Delta C_{\text{1M}}$) of $\mathbf{\$1,441.61\text{ USD}}$ ($\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$), $\mathbf{\$14,416.08\text{ USD}}$ ($\text{SF}=10\text{ GB}$), $\mathbf{\$43,248.25\text{ USD}}$ ($\text{SF}=30\text{ GB}$), and $\mathbf{\$144,160.82\text{ USD}}$ ($\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$) per million queries in V1, and $\$761.15\text{--}\$76,115.00\text{ USD}$ ($40.6\%$ pruning) in V2.

\subsection{PCIe Host-to-Device Bottleneck vs. SIMT GPU Acceleration}
Tables~4 and \ref{tab:throughput_and_cost}, alongside Fig.~\ref{fig:gpu_pcie_decomposition}, present the hardware-timed CUDA execution telemetry on the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 GPU ($4.0\text{ GB}$ VRAM, $N=375$ trials):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Break-Even Crossover Analysis of Tier L1 Scans:} In L1 scan-and-filter workloads ($\text{CSCI}=15.3$), pure in-VRAM GPU kernel execution requires $24.46\text{ ms}$ ($\Theta_{\text{eff}} = 13.28\text{ GB/s}$), whereas PCIe Host-to-Device transfer incurs $30.56\text{ ms}$, yielding a PCIe bottleneck fraction of $\Phi_{\text{PCIe}} = 55.5\%$. By Eq.~(3), the required break-even kernel speedup is $(1 - 0.555)^{-1} = \mathbf{2.25\times}$. Because L1's low arithmetic intensity yields a warm kernel speedup of only $2.08\times < 2.25\times$, cold-start GPU execution ($55.02\text{ ms}$) is slower than DuckDB CPU ($50.84\text{ ms}$, $0.92\times$), Polars ($38.00\text{ ms}$), and DataFusion ($33.58\text{ ms}$).
    \item \textbf{Non-Linear GPU Speedup in High-CSCI Workloads (L2--L5):} As structural query complexity increases ($\text{CSCI} \in [26.8, 37.1]$), cold-start GPU execution ($56.96\text{ ms}$ overall, 95\% CI: $[46.23, 68.44]\text{ ms}$; Cliff's $\delta = +0.199\text{ [Small]}, p = 5.22 \times 10^{-4}$ vs. DuckDB) outperforms DuckDB Native CPU by $\mathbf{2.52\times}$ in L2 multi-way joins ($104.88\text{ ms}$ vs. $264.53\text{ ms}$), $\mathbf{2.09\times}$ in L3 staged CTEs ($58.24\text{ ms}$ vs. $121.92\text{ ms}$), $\mathbf{4.03\times}$ in L4 multi-axis window sorting ($19.37\text{ ms}$ vs. $78.16\text{ ms}$), and $\mathbf{8.52\times}$ in L5 array unnesting ($47.31\text{ ms}$ vs. $403.27\text{ ms}$). When columnar tensors reside in warm VRAM ($22.26\text{ ms}$ overall, 95\% CI: $[16.78, 28.68]\text{ ms}$; Cliff's $\delta = +0.722\text{ [Large]}, p = 1.01 \times 10^{-12}$ vs. DuckDB), GPU kernel execution achieves a mean throughput of $\Theta_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{18.89\text{ GB/s}}$ (peaking at $\mathbf{27.20\text{ GB/s}}$ in L3 and $\mathbf{23.73\text{ GB/s}}$ in L4), outperforming the fastest vectorized CPU baseline (Polars, 82.47 ms) by $3.71\times$ warm and $1.45\times$ cold, and DuckDB Native CPU (183.74 ms total / 182.87 ms kernel) by $8.26\times$ warm ($8.22\times$ pure kernel-to-kernel) and $3.23\times$ cold.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Six-Software Cross-Paradigm Synthesis: Full Row-Store vs. C++ DBMS vs. Rust Arrow Engines}
Tables~\ref{tab:six_software_matrix} and \ref{tab:throughput_and_cost} and Fig.~\ref{fig:six_software_chart} synthesize execution characteristics across all six software engines under 100\% full-scale execution. An omnibus non-parametric Friedman test across the 75 queries confirms highly significant performance differentiation across the six engines ($\chi^2_F(5) = 321.50, p = 2.382 \times 10^{-67}$, Kendall's coefficient of concordance $W = 0.8573$), revealing three critical software engineering insights:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Two-Orders-of-Magnitude Superiority Over Full-Scale Row-Store Baselines:} Full-scale tuple-at-a-time B-tree execution in SQLite 3 incurs a mean latency of $8417.33\text{ ms}$ (95\% CI: $[6412.19, 10819.70]\text{ ms}$, $\Theta_{\text{eff}} = 0.049\text{ GB/s}$), peaking at $19374.0\text{ ms}$ ($\Theta_{\text{eff}} = 0.024\text{ GB/s}$) in L2 multi-way joins due to random B-tree page traversal across $6.00\times 10^6$ rows. Vectorized columnar CPU engines achieve complete stochastic dominance (Cliff's $\delta = +1.000\text{ [Large]}, p = 5.28 \times 10^{-14}$) with overall mean speedups of $45.8\times$ in DuckDB ($183.74\text{ ms}$), $87.4\times$ in Apache DataFusion ($96.30\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[78.31, 114.92]\text{ ms}$, $4.83\text{ GB/s}$), and $102.1\times$ in Polars ($82.47\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[67.93, 98.65]\text{ ms}$, $5.07\text{ GB/s}$; mean tier speedup $121.6\times$, peaking at $237.2\times$ in L2).
    \item \textbf{Zero-Copy Apache Arrow Advantage in Full-Table Unnesting (L1 \& L5):} On un-truncated $6.00\times 10^6$-row tables, Apache DataFusion ($33.58\text{ ms}$, $9.67\text{ GB/s}$ in L1; $110.31\text{ ms}$, $2.42\text{ GB/s}$ in L5) and Polars ($38.00\text{ ms}$ in L1; $154.76\text{ ms}$ in L5) outperform DuckDB ($50.84\text{ ms}$ in L1; $403.27\text{ ms}$ in L5) by $2.61\times\text{--}3.66\times$ in L5 array unnesting because Arrow \texttt{ListArray} structures maintain contiguous offset buffers in RAM. Meanwhile, in L5 full-table unnesting, GPU radix expansion ($47.31\text{ ms}$ cold, $12.84\text{ ms}$ warm at $20.79\text{ GB/s}$) surpasses all CPU engines.
    \item \textbf{C++ Segment-Tree/Streaming Window Optimization vs. Rust Sort-Based Windows (L4):} In L4 multi-axis window ranking and sliding-frame queries, Polars ($71.11\text{ ms}$, $3.32\text{ GB/s}$) and DuckDB ($78.16\text{ ms}$, $3.02\text{ GB/s}$) closely lead CPU execution, with DuckDB surpassing Apache DataFusion ($173.16\text{ ms}$, $1.36\text{ GB/s}$) by $\mathbf{2.22\times}$, reflecting the efficiency of DuckDB's parallel segment-tree window framing compared to DataFusion's sort-based partition evaluators. Meanwhile, GPU execution ($9.94\text{ ms}$ warm at $23.73\text{ GB/s}$, $19.37\text{ ms}$ cold) remains the fastest overall paradigm for L4 window workloads.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hardware Resource Utilization, Estimated CPU Package vs.\ Measured GPU Board Energy, and Energy-Aware Efficiency}
Table~\ref{tab:hardware_energy_telemetry} and Fig.~\ref{fig:hardware_energy_chart} report empirical hardware sensor telemetry across all 75 benchmark workloads, establishing three key systems-level insights:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Multi-Core SIMD Saturation vs. Single-Thread B-Tree Bottleneck:} SQLite 3 is inherently bound to single-thread tuple iteration, utilizing only $8.86\%$ mean CPU capacity ($2.26\text{ GB}$ RAM) while prolonging active package power draw over $8.42\text{ s}$, resulting in an active CPU energy footprint of $\mathbf{126.26\text{ J/query}}$ (peaking at $293.4\text{ J}$ in L2 joins) and a mere $\mathbf{4.05\text{ MB/J}}$ in performance per watt. Conversely, vectorized CPU engines effectively saturate multi-core execution pipelines---achieving mean CPU utilizations of $\mathbf{77.44\%}$ in Polars (peaking at $89.1\%$ in L2 joins), $\mathbf{70.12\%}$ in Apache DataFusion, and $\mathbf{66.10\%}$ in DuckDB within a $4.65\text{--}4.71\text{ GB}$ host RAM envelope.
    \item \textbf{Order-of-Magnitude Energy Reduction via Columnar Vectorization:} By compressing execution durations into tens of milliseconds, columnar CPU engines reduce mean per-query estimated CPU package energy ($E_{\text{CPU}}$) by $19.7\times\text{--}39.8\times$ compared to SQLite 3, requiring only $\mathbf{3.17\text{ J}}$ ($1.97\text{ J}$ net dynamic energy above idle) in Polars ($159.7\text{ MB/J}$), $\mathbf{3.39\text{ J}}$ ($2.00\text{ J}$ dynamic) in DataFusion ($157.1\text{ MB/J}$), and $\mathbf{6.40\text{ J}}$ ($3.73\text{ J}$ dynamic) in DuckDB ($243.0\text{ MB/J}$ overall and $515.2\text{ MB/J}$ in L1 scans).
    \item \textbf{Measured GPU Board vs. Estimated CPU Package Energy Efficiency:} On the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 GPU, relational tensor execution occupies a compact $\mathbf{1.30\text{ GB}}$ VRAM footprint ($32.5\%$ of the $4.0\text{ GB}$ device buffer) with a mean SM utilization of $\mathbf{31.04\%}$ across sub-$50\text{ ms}$ kernels. At the device board level, GPU execution consumes $\mathbf{0.65\text{ J/query}}$ ($0.14\text{ J}$ net dynamic energy) under cold-start PCIe transfers ($\mathbf{672.3\text{ MB/J}}$) and $\mathbf{0.25\text{ J/query}}$ ($0.05\text{ J}$ dynamic) in warm VRAM ($\mathbf{1942.8\text{ MB/J}}$, peaking at $2558.0\text{ MB/J}$ in L3 and $2502.1\text{ MB/J}$ in L4), representing a $\mathbf{4.9\times\text{--}9.8\times}$ cold-start device energy reduction ($12.7\times\text{--}25.6\times$ warm) relative to vectorized CPU package energy (or $2.0\times\text{--}4.1\times$ cold and $5.2\times\text{--}10.5\times$ warm when accounting for composite Host CPU + GPU board energy $E_{\text{sys}} = 1.58\text{ J}$ cold and $0.61\text{ J}$ warm).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Comparison Against Static Single-Engine Baselines (Models 1--3):} Relying on a single static engine incurs severe performance regret and resource penalties across heterogeneous workloads. \textbf{(1) Always DuckDB} achieves $28.00\%$ Top-1 and $49.33\%$ Top-2 accuracy but suffers from a $\mathbf{271.10\%}$ mean performance regret ($928.32\%$ P95 regret, $183.74\text{ ms}$ mean, $731.22\text{ ms}$ P95, $6.397\text{ J}$, $4.71\text{ GB}$ RAM) due to slower multi-way joins (L2) and array unnesting (L5). \textbf{(2) Always GPU (Cold-Start)} achieves $49.33\%$ Top-1 and $60.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy ($56.96\text{ ms}$ mean, $0.644\text{ J}$, $1.30\text{ GB}$ VRAM) but incurs a $\mathbf{60.76\%}$ mean regret and a $\mathbf{257.31\%}$ P95 tail regret ($620.63\%$ worst-case regret) because low-complexity L1 scans and narrow V1 joins are penalized by PCIe Host-to-Device transfer overhead. \textbf{(3) Always BigQuery} incurs a $745.39\text{ ms}$ mean latency ($2794.05\text{ ms}$ P95), a $\mathbf{1709.86\%}$ latency regret on $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$ workloads, $15.720\text{ J}$ of slot energy, and a cloud FinOps expenditure of $\mathbf{\$1267.66\text{ USD}}$ per million queries.
    \item \textbf{Comparison Against Rule-Based Routing and Top-2 / Regret Proof (Model 4 vs. Model 5):} \textbf{(4) Rule-Based Routing} (using fixed operator thresholds on $J, W, U, S$ in $34.07\text{ }\mu\text{s}$) improves upon static CPU engines ($71.48\text{ ms}$ mean, 95\% CI: $[52.79, 91.74]\text{ ms}$, $49.33\%$ Top-1, $65.33\%$ Top-2 accuracy, $65.63\%$ mean regret, $252.40\%$ P95 regret), while the 8-dimensional \emph{Syntax-Only AST Router} achieves $52.00\%$ Top-1 ($69.33\%$ Top-2) accuracy and $56.33\%$ regret ($78.31\text{ ms}$). \subsection{Internal Framework Validation: Feature Ablation, Multi-Objective Pareto Trade-off, Noise Robustness, and Overhead Breakdown}
To rigorously validate the internal mechanisms of \textbf{AST-Route}, Tables~9 and~14 and Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_ablation_pareto} report six additional verification experiments under 25-seed LOSO-CV:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Feature Group Ablation and Multicollinearity Defense (Exp A):} Using only basic tree size features (\textbf{A1}: $D(q), N(q)$) results in poor routing accuracy ($44.00\%$, $103.94\text{ ms}$, $137.15\%$ regret). Adding individual operator counts without CSCI or selectivity (\textbf{A2}: $7\text{D}$) reduces latency to $61.06\text{ ms}$ ($50.87\%$ regret). Remarkably, the 2-dimensional compact representation combining our proposed scalar index with selectivity (\textbf{A3}: $\text{CSCI}(q) + \sigma(q)$) achieves $\mathbf{56.30\text{ ms}}$ ($54.67\%$ Top-1 accuracy, $39.11\%$ regret, $1.173\text{ J}$), substantially outperforming both the 7D operator-only model (\textbf{A2}) and the 8D Syntax-Only AST model without selectivity (\textbf{A4}: $73.22\text{ ms}$, $65.78\%$ regret). Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) auditing confirms that the six atomic AST operators and $\sigma(q)$ exhibit low mutual collinearity (mean $\text{VIF} = 1.63$, max $\text{VIF} = 1.86 < 5.0$, orthogonalized $\text{CSCI}$ residual $\text{VIF} = 1.84$). Because Gradient Boosting Decision Trees partition features sequentially via non-linear axis-aligned splits, including $\text{CSCI}(q)$ alongside raw operator counts in \textbf{A5 (Full 9D Proposed AST-Route)} provides a pre-computed linear oblique hyperplane that reduces tree depth requirements and improves out-of-fold routing to the optimal $\mathbf{54.92\text{ ms}}$ latency and $\mathbf{34.98\%}$ regret.
    \item \textbf{Constrained 3D Latency--Energy--FinOps Pareto Frontier and Memory Pruning (Exp B):} Varying the normalized objective weights $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$ and hardware memory caps ($M_{\max}$) in Eq.~(8) enables principled navigation across latency, energy, memory, and cloud FinOps dimensions (Table 9 Exp B and Fig.~9b). At $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$, shifting from pure latency optimization in B1 ($(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,0,0)$: $54.92\text{ ms}$, $1.381\text{ J}$) to B2 ($(0.75, 0.25, 0)$) preserves virtually identical mean latency ($54.99\text{ ms}$) while reducing P95 tail latency to $124.13\text{ ms}$ and cutting energy by $14.4\%$ ($1.182\text{ J}$), whereas B3 ($(0.50, 0.50, 0)$), B4 ($(0.25, 0.75, 0)$), and B5 ($(0, 1, 0)$) progressively compress energy to $1.007\text{ J}$, $0.879\text{ J}$, and $0.748\text{ J}$ ($-45.8\%$). When a strict GPU memory budget of $M_{\text{gpu\_max}} \le 1.28\text{ GB}$ is enforced in B6 ($(0.60, 0.20, 0.20)$), the feasibility filter $\mathcal{E}_{\text{feasible}}(q)$ automatically prunes GPU offloading on L2--L4 queries whose predicted VRAM footprint ($1.33\text{ GB}$) exceeds the cap, routing them to Polars/DuckDB to maintain $1.24\text{ GB}$ peak VRAM with zero OOM violations ($64.18\text{ ms}$, $1.842\text{ J}$). Furthermore, at $\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$ where local CPU engines hit the $9.0\text{ GB}$ RAM cap ($3302\text{ MB}$ disk spill), the Cloud-Elastic policy B7 ($(0.80, 0.10, 0.10)$) offloads spill-bound L1/L4 queries to BigQuery to achieve $1142.8\text{ ms}$ ($19.40\%$ regret, $\$507.06/1\text{M}$ queries), whereas the FinOps-Constrained policy B8 ($(0.40, 0.20, 0.40), C_{\max}=\$0$) eliminates cloud expenditure entirely ($\$0.00/1\text{M}$) while sustaining $1326.9\text{ ms}$ via chunked local GPU/CPU execution (with cloud pricing sensitivity sweeps across $\$3.125\text{--}\$12.50\text{/TiB}$ confirming smooth Pareto adaptation from $1118.4\text{ ms}$ to $1245.6\text{ ms}$). 
    \item \textbf{Selectivity Estimation Noise Robustness and Sample Efficiency (Exp C \& Exp D):} Under 50-trial Monte Carlo uniform multiplicative noise injected into test-time selectivity estimates ($\pm 10\%, \pm 25\%,$ and $\pm 50\%$ in \textbf{C2--C4}), \textbf{AST-Route} maintains $52.80\%\text{--}53.79\%$ Top-1 accuracy ($58.19\%\text{--}60.51\%$ near-optimal hit rate) and $75.00\text{--}80.98\text{ ms}$ mean latency, remaining faster than all static CPU baselines even under $\pm 50\%$ selectivity drift. Furthermore, cold-start learning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_ablation_pareto}c) demonstrate that training on just $5$ canonical seeds ($15$ queries) achieves $63.61\text{ ms}$ ($51.38\%$ regret), already surpassing Rule-Based Routing ($71.48\text{ ms}$), and converges to $56.59\text{ ms}$ by $15$ seeds ($45$ queries). Moreover, when zone-map metadata is deliberately masked on $50\%$ of test queries (simulating incomplete catalog statistics on external tables and falling back to default parametric operator priors without executing the query), AST-Route sustains $53.33\%$ Top-1 accuracy and $68.42\text{ ms}$ mean latency---confirming genuine pre-execution operation independent of actual cardinality.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Router Overhead Breakdown (Exp E):} Across all 75 SQL files, Stage 1 \texttt{SQLGlot} AST parsing averages $3.476\text{ ms}$ ($3475.91\text{ }\mu\text{s}$), Stage 2 9D feature and zone-map extraction averages $0.172\text{ ms}$ ($172.04\text{ }\mu\text{s}$), and Stage 3 Multi-Output GBR inference averages $0.497\text{ ms}$ ($497.16\text{ }\mu\text{s}$), yielding a total end-to-end framework overhead of $\mathbf{4.145\text{ ms}}$ ($7.55\%$ of mean execution time). Because our reported $54.92\text{ ms}$ AST-Route latency already includes this $4.145\text{ ms}$ warm overhead ($50.775\text{ ms}$ engine execution $+ 4.145\text{ ms}$ router overhead; $55.16\text{ ms}$ under cold NVMe footer seek), net end-to-end AST-Route execution significantly outperforms Rule-Based Routing ($71.48\text{ ms}$), Always Polars ($82.47\text{ ms}$), Always DataFusion ($96.30\text{ ms}$), and Always DuckDB ($183.74\text{ ms}$, a $3.35\times$ net end-to-end speedup).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cost Prediction Q-Error, Regret Distribution, Tier-by-Tier Rule Comparison, and Unseen Seed Generalization}
Table~11 and Fig.~\ref{fig:priority3_unseen_cost} present the out-of-sample validation experiments examining cost-model precision, regret quantiles, tier-level behavior against heuristics, and generalization to strictly unseen query seeds:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Multi-Output Cost and Resource Prediction Accuracy (Table~11, Part A):} Evaluated across all 75 queries out-of-fold, Layer 3 achieves high predictive fidelity across the primary local and GPU execution targets: on CUDA GPU cold-start latency, the model achieves $\log\text{-}R^2 = \mathbf{0.875}$, $\text{MAPE} = \mathbf{13.34\%}$, a median multiplicative Q-Error of $\text{P50} = \mathbf{1.053}$ ($5.3\%$ median deviation from ground truth), and a 90th-percentile Q-Error of $\text{P90} = \mathbf{1.371}$ (mean predicted $57.16\text{ ms}$ vs. true $56.96\text{ ms}$). On Polars and DataFusion, median Q-Errors are $\mathbf{1.095}$ ($\text{MAPE}=16.79\%, R^2=0.822$) and $\mathbf{1.105}$ ($\text{MAPE}=23.82\%, R^2=0.594$), respectively. For per-query energy footprint ($\text{Joules}$) and host working-set memory ($\text{GB}$), median Q-Errors reach $\mathbf{1.172}$ ($R^2=0.815$) and $\mathbf{1.007}$ ($\text{MAPE}=2.21\%$). While BigQuery exhibits higher relative variance ($\text{P50}=1.326, R^2=0.462$) due to multi-tenant cloud slot scheduling jitter, its latency floor at $\text{SF}=1\text{--}10\text{ GB}$ ($745\text{--}1252\text{ ms}$) is separated by an order of magnitude from local engines ($55\text{--}195\text{ ms}$), ensuring zero false-positive cloud dispatches at small scales while accurately triggering cloud fallback when local out-of-core spilling dominates at $\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$. To explicitly guard against cost-prediction uncertainty across heterogeneous engines (where out-of-fold 90\% conformal prediction intervals achieve $88.0\%\text{--}94.7\%$ empirical coverage with half-widths of $\pm 11.4\text{ ms}$ on GPU, $\pm 14.8\text{ ms}$ on Polars, and $\pm 318.5\text{ ms}$ on BigQuery), AST-Route enforces confidence-aware abstention (Table 11 Part C): the dispatcher abstains from GPU offloading when the predicted latency advantage over vectorized CPU falls within the $12\%$ PCIe uncertainty margin and abstains from cloud offloading unless BigQuery's upper 90\% prediction bound beats local execution. Evaluating risk-based routing metrics across all 75 queries confirms that when the cost model is uncertain, defaulting to a safer deterministic CPU engine substantially outperforms naive point-estimate $\arg\min$ selection: across the six candidate targets in Part A, the 90\% conformal prediction intervals achieve $88.0\%\text{--}94.7\%$ empirical coverage (mean coverage $90.9\%$, Expected Calibration Error $\text{ECE}=1.78\%$). By abstaining from risky GPU PCIe transfers on $16.00\%$ ($12/75$) of TPC-H queries ($21.21\%$ [$7/33$] on JOB) where predicted GPU and CPU latencies overlap within $12\%$, risk-based confidence-aware routing reduces the probability of selecting a slower engine ($P_{\text{subopt}} = 1 - \text{Zero-Regret Hit Rate}$) from $46.67\%$ ($35/75$) under point-estimate $\arg\min$ to $41.33\%$ ($31/75$), lowers expected latency regret from $48.12\%$ ($58.64\text{ ms}$) to $34.98\%$ ($54.92\text{ ms}$), cuts P95 tail regret from $164.80\%$ to $114.11\%$, reduces worst-case (maximum) regret from $312.40\%$ to $245.18\%$ (vs.\ $620.63\%$ on Always GPU), more than halves the high-regret failure rate ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{regret}} > 100\%$) from $14.67\%$ ($11/75$) to $6.67\%$ ($5/75$), and elevates routing decision stability across repeated trials from $93.33\%$ to $98.67\%$.
    \item \textbf{Generalization to Strictly Unseen Query Seeds and External Cross-Schema Workloads (JOB \& SSB, Table 11 Part B):} When trained strictly on the 20 development seeds (\texttt{S1}--\texttt{S4}, $N_{\text{dev}}=60$ queries) and evaluated on the 5 locked unseen test seeds (\texttt{L1\_S5}--\texttt{L5\_S5}, $N_{\text{unseen}}=15$ queries), AST-Route ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$) achieves a $73.33\%$ Top-1 routing accuracy ($73.33\%$ zero-regret exact hits), a mean execution latency of $47.01\text{ ms}$ (approaching the Oracle lower bound of $41.30\text{ ms}$), and a 95th-percentile tail latency of $79.64\text{ ms}$---matching the exact P95 tail latency of the Oracle Ideal Router ($79.64\text{ ms}$). On these unseen seeds, AST-Route outperforms Always DuckDB ($210.55\text{ ms}$, $385.97\%$ regret) by $4.48\times$, Always Polars ($81.49\text{ ms}$, $164.66\%$ regret) by $1.73\times$, Always GPU ($54.78\text{ ms}$, $118.57\%$ regret) by $1.17\times$, and Rule-Based Routing ($64.17\text{ ms}$, P95: $168.59\text{ ms}$) by $1.37\times$ while cutting P95 tail latency by $52.8\%$. Furthermore, to test strict zero-shot cross-schema transfer without retraining or hyperparameter tuning, we locked the $\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$ model trained exclusively on the 75 TPC-H queries and evaluated it across two external multi-join benchmarks (Table 11 Part B): (i) the 33 canonical join-order templates (\texttt{1a}--\texttt{33a}, 4-way to 10-way cyclic/snowflake joins over 21 tables, mean $J=7.00$, mean $\text{CSCI}=30.50$) of the Join Order Benchmark (JOB) [27] on the real-world IMDB dataset ($\sim 3.6\text{ GB}$), and (ii) all 13 standard star-join queries (\texttt{Q1.1}--\texttt{Q4.3} across Flights 1--4, 1-way to 5-way joins over \texttt{lineorder}, \texttt{customer}, \texttt{supplier}, \texttt{part}, and \texttt{date}, mean $J=3.15$, mean $\text{CSCI}=24.19$) of the Star Schema Benchmark (SSB) [26] at $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$ ($6.0\times 10^6$ \texttt{lineorder} rows, translated deterministically across engines via \texttt{SQLGlot}). Using individual queries as the unit of analysis for 10,000-iteration paired bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals and two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm--Bonferroni step-down correction across multiple engine comparisons, we observe distinct, complementary generalization behaviors across the two external schemas. On JOB ($N=33$), because the confidence-aware dispatcher abstains from GPU offloading under narrow/uncertain string-filtered join margins, AST-Route selects Polars across all $33/33$ templates, yielding an identical execution profile that matches Always Polars ($6.75\text{ ms}$ mean, $6.52\text{ ms}$ median, $10.51\text{ ms}$ P95, paired difference $\Delta T = 0.00\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[0.00, 0.00]\text{ ms}$, $p=1.000$). We emphasize that AST-Route does not outperform Always Polars on JOB; rather, its value lies in autonomously identifying Polars as the safe, low-variance choice without prior IMDB profiling. Because Cold GPU ($9.05\text{ ms}$ mean, $4.45\text{ ms}$ median, $29.26\text{ ms}$ P95, $124.57\%$ mean regret) is the true Top-1 Oracle on $26/33$ in-VRAM join queries (where Polars ranks second within $1.4\text{--}2.3\text{ ms}$), selecting Polars yields a $21.21\%$ Top-1 accuracy ($7/33$ queries), a $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy ($33/33$ queries), a $21.21\%$ near-optimal ($\le 5\%$) hit rate, a $30.30\%$ near-optimal ($\le 10\%$) hit rate ($10/33$ queries), a $43.84\%$ median regret, an $84.49\%$ mean regret ($181.2\%$ P95 regret), and a small absolute latency gap of $+2.72\text{ ms}$ relative to the $4.03\text{ ms}$ Oracle. Crucially, on the remaining $7/33$ highly selective string-filtered queries (\texttt{LIKE '\%...\%'}), avoiding GPU offloading prevents severe $19.8\text{--}26.1\text{ ms}$ PCIe transfer stalls, enabling AST-Route to statistically outperform Always GPU by $1.34\times$ (query-level paired $\Delta T = -2.30\text{ ms}$, 10,000-bootstrap 95\% CI: $[-3.42, -1.21]\text{ ms}$, two-sided Wilcoxon $p=0.0398$), Always DuckDB ($19.55\text{ ms}$, $2.90\times$, paired $\Delta T = -12.80\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[-14.85, -10.74]\text{ ms}$, $p=5.39\times 10^{-7}$, Holm--Bonferroni $p_{\text{Holm}}=6.98\times 10^{-10}$), and Always DataFusion ($31.25\text{ ms}$, $4.63\times$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=6.98\times 10^{-10}$). Conversely, on the external SSB star-schema workload ($N=13$ queries, \texttt{Q1.1}--\texttt{Q4.3}) where numeric and date range predicates align with zone-map selectivity $\sigma(q)$, zero-shot AST-Route dynamically dispatches Flight 1 and selective Flight 2/3 queries (\texttt{Q1.1}--\texttt{Q1.3}, \texttt{Q2.2}--\texttt{Q2.3}, \texttt{Q3.3}--\texttt{Q3.4}) to Polars Arrow and compute-heavy 3-to-5-way star joins (\texttt{Q2.1}, \texttt{Q3.1}--\texttt{Q3.2}, \texttt{Q4.1}--\texttt{Q4.3}) to the GPU, achieving $92.31\%$ Top-1 accuracy ($12/13$ queries), $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy ($13/13$ queries), $92.31\%$ near-optimal ($\le 5\%$ and $\le 10\%$) hit rates, a $12.15\text{ ms}$ mean latency ($11.85\text{ ms}$ median, $18.79\text{ ms}$ P95 vs.\ $11.75\text{ ms}$ Oracle, $+0.40\text{ ms}$ absolute latency gap), $0.00\%$ median regret, and $2.82\%$ mean regret---statistically outperforming Always Polars ($13.75\text{ ms}$, $1.13\times$), Always GPU ($13.91\text{ ms}$, $1.14\times$, paired $\Delta T = -1.76\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[-3.18, -0.42]\text{ ms}$), and Always DuckDB ($21.17\text{ ms}$, $1.74\times$, paired $\Delta T = -9.02\text{ ms}$, 95\% CI: $[-11.60, -6.58]\text{ ms}$, Wilcoxon $p=0.00024$). Because JOB ($N=33$) and SSB ($N=13$) represent finite benchmark suites, these results demonstrate bounded cross-schema generalization across star and snowflake analytical topologies rather than universal optimality across arbitrary production workloads.
\item \textbf{Regret Quantile Distribution and Tier-by-Tier Comparison Against Rule-Based Heuristics (Table~11, Part C):} Across the full 75-query workload, \textbf{AST-Route} achieves a median (P50) regret of $\mathbf{0.00\%}$ ($58.67\%$ zero-regret exact hits) and compresses 90th-percentile (P90) tail regret to $\mathbf{89.75\%}$, compared to $215.57\%$ for Rule-Based Routing ($2.40\times$ tail regret reduction) and $174.03\%$ for Always GPU. Tier-by-tier analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:priority3_unseen_cost}b) reveals why learned cost regression outperforms static heuristics: while Rule-Based Routing matches \textbf{AST-Route} in L4 window functions ($19.37\text{ ms}, 100.0\%$ accuracy), static rules fail severely in L5 array unnesting ($145.62\text{ ms}, 225.86\%$ regret, $0.0\%$ Top-1 accuracy) by routing full-table $6.00\times 10^6$-row \texttt{UNNEST} queries to CPU Arrow engines. By contrast, \textbf{AST-Route} predicts that SIMT GPU radix expansion amortizes PCIe transfer costs in L5, reducing mean latency to $\mathbf{51.51\text{ ms}}$ ($23.57\%$ regret, $\mathbf{2.83\times}$ faster than Rule-Based Routing).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Multi-Scale Big Data Evaluation ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30, 100\text{ GB}$), Memory/VRAM Walls, Cross-Platform, Concurrency, and Nested LOO-CV on Official TPC-H Q1--Q22}
Tables~\ref{tab:bigdata_multiscale_tpch} and 13 and Fig.~\ref{fig:bigdata_multiscale} report the multi-scale Big Data experiments across the four standard TPC-H scale factors ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30, \text{and } 100\text{ GB}$) \cite{tpc2022tpch, dreseler2020quantifying}, three hardware platform profiles, multi-tenant concurrent streams ($C=1\text{--}32$), and the 22 official TPC-H benchmark queries (Q1--Q22):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Unified Four-Scale Memory-Hierarchy Transitions and Out-of-Core Disk Spilling ($\text{SF}=1 \rightarrow 10 \rightarrow 30 \rightarrow 100\text{ GB}$, Table~\ref{tab:bigdata_multiscale_tpch} Part A):} Scaling the full 75-query workload baseline across two orders of magnitude exposes three distinct hardware regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:bigdata_multiscale}a--b): (i) at $\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$ ($6.0\text{M}$ \texttt{lineitem} rows), working sets fit entirely in memory ($4.71\text{ GB}$ RAM, $0.0\text{ MB}$ disk spill, $1.30\text{ GB}$ VRAM, $1\times$ PCIe transfer), with \textbf{AST-Route} achieving $\mathbf{54.92\text{ ms}}$ ($3.35\times$ faster than DuckDB at $183.74\text{ ms}$ and $1.50\times$ faster than Polars at $82.47\text{ ms}$); (ii) at $\text{SF}=10\text{ GB}$ ($60.0\text{M}$ rows), data exceeds the $4.0\text{ GB}$ GPU VRAM ceiling, requiring $4\times$ chunked PCIe streaming ($3.45\text{ GB}$ peak VRAM, $195.03\text{ ms}$ on GPU vs. $950.88\text{ ms}$ on DuckDB and $656.75\text{ ms}$ on Polars) while host RAM reaches $7.85\text{ GB}$ with minor spilling ($125.0\text{ MB}$), where \textbf{AST-Route} achieves $\mathbf{182.40\text{ ms}}$ ($\mathbf{5.21\times}$ speedup over DuckDB); (iii) at $\text{SF}=30\text{ GB}$ ($180.0\text{M}$ rows), local engines hit the $9.00\text{ GB}$ DBMS memory cap, triggering active out-of-core disk spilling \cite{kuiper2024duckdb_ooc} ($864.0\text{ MB}$ mean spill, $2594.49\text{ ms}$ on DuckDB, $1877.63\text{ ms}$ on Polars) and $12\times$ PCIe chunking on the GPU ($3.68\text{ GB}$ VRAM, $481.85\text{ ms}$), where \textbf{AST-Route} achieves $\mathbf{448.65\text{ ms}}$ ($\mathbf{5.78\times}$ speedup over DuckDB); and (iv) at $\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$ ($600.1\text{M}$ \texttt{lineitem} rows, $866.1\text{M}$ total rows), extreme disk spilling ($3302.0\text{ MB}$ mean, $6840.0\text{ MB}$ in L2) slows local CPU engines to $6551.42\text{--}8699.98\text{ ms}$ ($256.20\text{--}385.40\text{ J}$). At $\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$, Google BigQuery Serverless Cloud ($2571.60\text{ ms}$ overall; $1493.51\text{ ms}$ in L1, $1700.85\text{ ms}$ in L3, $1286.16\text{ ms}$ in L4) overtakes all local CPU engines by $\mathbf{2.55\times\text{--}3.38\times}$ via horizontal slot elasticity, while \textbf{AST-Route} achieves $\mathbf{1142.80\text{ ms}}$ overall ($\mathbf{7.61\times}$ faster than DuckDB).

Furthermore, under multi-tenant concurrent query streams ($C=1, 4, 8, 16,$ and $32$ concurrent queries in Table 13 Part C, explicitly separating active execution time from admission queuing delay), single-engine CPU execution suffers from memory-bus contention ($85.62 \rightarrow 228.58\text{ ms}$ at $C=16$ and $442.80\text{ ms}$ at $C=32$, plateauing at $58.79\text{--}61.40\text{ QPS}$), whereas AST-Route asynchronously dispatches scan/join streams across CPU cores ($94.1\%\text{--}98.2\%$ util) and GPU SIMT queues ($86.8\%\text{--}93.5\%$ util) simultaneously---cutting batch latency to $114.30\text{ ms}$ ($68.10\text{ ms}$ active execution + $46.20\text{ ms}$ queuing, $139.99\text{ QPS}$, $2.00\times$ gain) at $C=16$ and $207.20\text{ ms}$ ($82.40\text{ ms}$ execution + $124.80\text{ ms}$ queuing, P95: $268.5\text{ ms}$, P99: $312.6\text{ ms}$, $154.44\text{ QPS}$, $2.14\times$ gain) at $C=32$ with $0.0\%$ failure rate and $96.0\%\text{--}100.0\%$ routing stability (compressing P50/P90/P95/P99 tail latencies at $C=32$ from $395.0/612.4/698.5/842.0\text{ ms}$ on static CPU to $178.5/244.1/268.5/312.6\text{ ms}$ on AST-Route).

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
To ensure a balanced interpretation of our findings, we categorize the limitations and threats to validity across three dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Hardware Envelope, GPU Tensor Backend Scope, and CPU Energy Estimation Limitations:} Our local multi-scale experiments ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30, \text{and } 100\text{ GB}$) were conducted within a $4.0\text{ GB}$ discrete GPU VRAM envelope (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 over PCIe Gen4) and a $16\text{ GB}$ host RAM envelope ($9.0\text{ GB}$ DBMS cap). Enterprise data center GPUs equipped with NVLink or HBM3e memory (e.g., NVIDIA H100/B200 with $80\text{--}192\text{ GB}$ VRAM) would eliminate multi-chunk PCIe streaming at $\text{SF}=10\text{--}100\text{ GB}$ and shift the PCIe break-even threshold ($\Phi_{\text{PCIe}}$) toward lower-CSCI queries. Additionally, while GPU power was sampled directly via hardware NVML board sensors, CPU package energy on Windows was estimated via TDP-calibrated utilization polling; integrating hardware Intel RAPL / AMD SMU ring-0 energy counters and external wall-socket power meters on Linux servers is an important direction for future systems telemetry. Specifically, while GPU board energy ($E_{\text{GPU}}$) is measured directly via physical NVIDIA NVML sensors ($\text{MAE}=0.008\text{ J}$, $\text{MACE}=1.24\%$), our CPU energy figures ($E_{\text{CPU}}$) represent \emph{Estimated CPU Package Energy} derived from TDP-calibrated utilization polling (evaluated across $R=50$ repetitions per workload tier against a non-linear DVFS curve with $\text{MAE}=0.098\text{--}0.205\text{ J}$, $\text{MACE}=3.09\%\text{--}3.21\%$, and Bland--Altman 95\% LoA within $\pm 0.42\text{ J}$) rather than ring-0 Intel RAPL / AMD SMU hardware counter readings or external wall-socket power meter measurements (which also capture PSU, DRAM, cooling, and NVMe storage rails). Accordingly, all energy optimizations in AST-Route are framed as \emph{energy-aware routing} over estimated CPU package and measured GPU board telemetry rather than certified whole-system wall-plug energy reductions. Similarly, our PyTorch CUDA engine serves as a compiled relational tensor execution backend (modeling core scan, radix hash-join, segmented group-by, bitonic sort, window prefix-scan, and array unnest primitives) rather than a monolithic SQL DBMS with a full query optimizer, catalog manager, or general correlated-subquery engine; integrating native GPU SQL engines such as Sirius [34] or HeavyDB [24] across broader hardware tiers is an important next step.
    \item \textbf{Workload, Schema, and Data-Distribution Skew Threats:} Although our evaluation spans 163 distinct SQL queries in total---comprising 97 core TPC-H benchmark queries (our 75-query factorial benchmark across L1--L5 and V1--V3 plus the 22 official TPC-H queries Q1--Q22, including complex 5-way to 8-way join graphs in Q2, Q5, Q7, Q8, Q9, and Q21 [28, 30]) and 66 external cross-schema queries (33 IMDB JOB, 13 SSB, and 20 ClickBench)---the underlying tables of the core suite follow the uniform key distributions of the canonical TPC-H generator. Real-world production workloads such as the Join Order Benchmark (JOB) \cite{leis2015job} over the IMDB dataset exhibit severe zipfian data skew and string-heavy predicates that can induce hash-join partition skew and cardinality estimation errors. Because fixed-scale join benchmarks such as JOB ($\sim 3.6\text{ GB}$ IMDB) contain neither discrete multi-scale tiers ($\text{SF} \in \{1, 10, 30, 100\}\text{ GB}$) nor window/UNNEST operators ($W(q)=0, U(q)=0$) and rely on substring predicates (\texttt{LIKE '\%...\%'}) rather than numeric/date zone maps, we preserved strict architectural symmetry across our four-scale memory-wall evaluation by validating external generalization on locked unseen seeds ($73.33\%$ Top-1) and the 22 official TPC-H choke-point queries (which already stress 5-way to 8-way join topologies in Q2, Q5, Q7, Q8, Q9, and Q21, achieving $90.91\%$ Top-2 accuracy under nested LOO-CV); moreover, our zero-shot cross-schema experiment (training strictly on TPC-H and testing on 33 JOB templates in Table 11 Part B) confirms that while AST-Route achieves $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy and the fastest overall mean latency ($6.75\text{ ms}$, beating DuckDB by $2.90\times$ and GPU by $1.34\times$), its Top-1 accuracy drops to $21.21\%$ due to unmodeled string-predicate skew between Polars and GPU; integrating string-skew histograms into $\mathbf{x}_q$ to further sharpen Top-1 discrimination on skewed schemas is a primary avenue for future work.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic State Contention in Distributed Multi-Node Clusters:} While we validated multi-tenant concurrency up to $C=32$ parallel query streams on a single heterogeneous node ($139.99\text{ QPS}$ / $2.00\times$ gain at $C=16$ and $154.44\text{ QPS}$ / $2.14\times$ gain at $C=32$ in Table 13 Part C), production deployments across multi-node distributed clusters (such as ClickHouse \cite{schulze2024clickhouse} and Trino \cite{sethi2019presto}) introduce network-fabric congestion, dynamic VRAM fragmentation, and admission-control queuing. Extending \textbf{AST-Route} into a stateful, queue-aware cluster scheduler across distributed nodes is a promising direction for future research.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We presented AST-Route, an interpretable, risk-aware multi-objective query routing framework grounded in empirical CPU--GPU--cloud crossover profiling and evaluated across 163 SQL queries in total---comprising 97 core TPC-H benchmark queries (a 75-query AST-stratified factorial workload across L1--L5 and V1--V3 plus the 22 official standard TPC-H queries Q1--Q22 [28]) and 66 external zero-shot cross-schema validation queries (33 IMDB JOB templates [27], 13 SSB star-join queries [26], and 20 ClickBench web-analytics queries)---across four standard scale factors ($\text{SF}=1, 10, 30,$ and $100\text{ GB}$ from 6.0M to 600.1M \texttt{lineitem} rows), three cross-platform hardware profiles, $C=1\text{--}32$ concurrent query streams, and six software systems across four computing paradigms: SQLite~3, Google BigQuery, DuckDB, Polars, Apache DataFusion, and CUDA GPU execution (NVIDIA RTX~2050). Validated via 10,000-iteration seed-clustered bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals, Cliff's delta effect sizes, 10,000-trial CSCI sensitivity analysis ($\bar{\rho}=0.9880$ at $\pm 20\%$, partial correlation $\rho_{\text{partial}}=0.5477$), strict GPU/CPU FP64 fairness protocols, feature group ablation, Pareto trade-off analysis, leak-free 25-seed LOSO-CV, unseen seed holdout testing, and strict nested 22-fold LOO-CV, our pre-execution 9D/10D feature representation ($\mathbf{x}_q\in\mathbb{R}^9$, extended to $\mathbf{x}_q^{\text{scale}}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$) enables multi-engine cost prediction (median Q-Error $1.007\text{--}1.172$, $R^2=0.815\text{--}0.875$) in $497.2\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ ($4.145\text{ ms}$ total warm overhead; $4.386\text{ ms}$ cold NVMe footer seek) and supports tunable operational trade-offs: point-estimate Ridge ($49.12\text{ ms}$), shallow decision tree ($51.35\text{ ms}$), $k$-NN ($52.84\text{ ms}$), and in-fold rule ($53.07\text{ ms}$) routers minimize raw mean latency at elevated P95/P99 tail regret ($150.9\%\text{--}196.8\%$ / $268.4\%\text{--}342.1\%$), whereas conformal AST-Route ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$) trades $3.6\text{--}5.8\text{ ms}$ of mean latency ($54.92\text{ ms}$ warm, $55.16\text{ ms}$ cold footer, $57.33\%$ Top-1 and $76.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy, $0.00\%$ median regret, $34.98\%$ mean regret, $1.381\text{ J}$ estimated device energy) to compress P95/P99 tail regret to $114.11\%$/$218.40\%$ and halve severe ($>100\%$ regret) routing failures from $14.67\%$ to $6.67\%$. Standalone CPU-only routing ($M_{\text{gpu\_max}}=0$) achieves $74.67\%$ Top-1 ($69.12\text{ ms}$) on the primary workstation and $86.67\%$ Top-1 ($62.05\text{ ms}$) on a 16-core node. Under independent seed-level ($N=25$ clusters) Holm--Bonferroni inference, AST-Route significantly outperforms Always DuckDB ($3.35\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=5.45\times 10^{-4}$), Always DataFusion ($1.75\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0041$), Always Polars ($1.50\times, p_{\text{Holm}}=0.0171$), and Rule-Based Routing ($p=0.0187$), while matching Always GPU in overall mean latency ($54.92$ vs.\ $56.96\text{ ms}$, seed-level $p=0.184$) yet cutting P95 tail regret from $257.31\%$ to $114.11\%$. Furthermore, AST-Route achieves $73.33\%$ Top-1 accuracy ($47.01\text{ ms}$) on locked unseen query seeds, $68.18\%$ Top-1 and $90.91\%$ Top-2 accuracy ($16.98\text{ ms}$, $2.52\times$ faster than Always Polars) under nested LOO-CV on official TPC-H Q1--Q22, safe zero-shot cross-schema transfer across 66 external queries (matching Always Polars at $6.75\text{ ms}$ with $21.21\%$ Top-1, $100.00\%$ Top-2, and $84.49\%$ mean regret on 33 JOB queries while beating DuckDB by $2.90\times$ and Always GPU by $1.34\times$; achieving $92.31\%$ Top-1 / $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy with $12.15\text{ ms}$ mean latency and $2.82\%$ mean regret on 13 SSB queries; and achieving $80.00\%$ Top-1 / $95.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy with $24.18\text{ ms}$ mean latency and $9.45\%$ mean regret on 20 ClickBench queries), $1.14\times\text{--}2.14\times$ steady-state concurrent throughput gains ($13.13\text{--}154.44\text{ QPS}$ across $C=1\text{--}32$), and $3.35\times\text{--}7.61\times$ speedups over DuckDB across $\text{SF}=1\text{--}100\text{ GB}$. Synthesizing our empirical findings across all seven architectural dimensions, this study establishes three core methodological conclusions: (i) \textit{Incremental Value of CSCI and Explicit Latency--Tail Risk Control}: engineered oblique hyperplane projection ($\text{CSCI}_{\text{res}}$, $\text{VIF}=1.84$) reduces active tree splits by $-28.2\%$ and lowers out-of-fold routing regret by $-9.22\%$ ($p=0.0038$) beyond raw AST operator counts, while pairing $\mathcal{R}_{\text{GBR-9D}}$ with calibrated conformal intervals protects tail SLAs near PCIe crossover boundaries; (ii) \textit{Operator-Bounded Cross-Workload Transfer}: AST-Route transfers effectively to in-distribution unseen seeds ($73.33\%$ Top-1), official TPC-H choke points ($90.91\%$ Top-2), star-join schemas (SSB: $92.31\%$ Top-1 and $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy at $2.82\%$ mean regret), wide-table web analytics (ClickBench: $80.00\%$ Top-1 and $95.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy at $24.18\text{ ms}$), and string-skewed cyclic joins via conservative conformal abstention (JOB: $100.00\%$ Top-2 accuracy, matching Always Polars at $6.75\text{ ms}$), whereas holding out entire unseen operator classes under Leave-One-Tier-Out (LOTO-CV) degrades latency to $85.42\text{ ms}$ (trailing Always Polars at $82.47\text{ ms}$), confirming that training workloads must span the target SQL operator families; and (iii) \textit{Memory-Constrained Multi-Scale and Energy-Aware Dispatch}: coupling zone-map selectivity with catalog byte footprints ($\mathcal{B}_{\text{catalog}}$) enables seamless routing across in-VRAM GPU execution ($\text{SF}=1\text{ GB}$), chunked PCIe streaming ($\text{SF}=10\text{--}30\text{ GB}$), and elastic serverless cloud offloading under heavy host disk spilling ($\text{SF}=100\text{ GB}$) while jointly balancing latency, estimated device-level energy, and marginal cloud scan charges.
